# Koch’s Postulates

## One-Sentence Definition

Koch’s postulates are a set of criteria used to establish that a specific microorganism causes a specific disease.

## Simple Explanation

To prove “this microbe causes this disease,” you should find it in the sick, isolate it pure, reproduce the disease in a host, and recover the same microbe again.

## Detailed Scientific Explanation

Formulated in the context of [[Robert Koch]]’s work on anthrax and tuberculosis, the classical postulates are:

1. The microorganism is found in abundance in all organisms suffering from the disease, but not in healthy organisms.
2. The microorganism can be isolated from a diseased organism and grown in pure culture.
3. The cultured microorganism should cause disease when introduced into a healthy organism.
4. The microorganism must be re-isolated from the inoculated, diseased experimental host and identified as identical to the original.

Modern practice recognizes exceptions: asymptomatic carriage, unculturable organisms, ethical limits on human challenge, opportunistic pathogens, toxins without ongoing infection, and polymicrobial disease. **Molecular Koch’s postulates** (Falkow and later frameworks) replace or supplement culture/challenge with evidence that a gene or factor is required for virulence.

## Mechanism

Operational logic: association → isolation → causation via challenge → confirmation by re-isolation. Each step reduces alternative explanations (contamination, coincidence, secondary invaders).

## Clinical Importance

- Still the mental model behind “prove the etiology.”
- Explains why labs chase culture/ID and why syndromes need careful interpretation when only PCR is positive.

## Research Importance

- Template for pathogen discovery and virulence-factor validation.
- Guides genomic epidemiology: presence of organism/gene vs causal role.

## Diagnostic Relevance

- Pure culture remains a gold-standard pillar when feasible.
- Molecular assays ([[PCR]], WGS) expand detection but do not automatically satisfy classical causation.

## AMR Relevance

- Distinguishing colonizer vs pathogen matters for whether resistance genes in a isolate are clinically actionable.

### Clinical Example
> [!example]
> **Case:** Multiplex PCR on a nasal swab is positive for *S. pneumoniae* in a patient with viral bronchiolitis and a clear chest X-ray.
> **Question:** Which Koch step is unsatisfied if you claim pneumococcus caused pneumonia?
> **Answer:** Association without disease (and no isolation from a sterile/diseased site). DNA presence ≠ etiology — see [[PCR]] colonization section and [[Figure - Koch Postulates Flow]].
